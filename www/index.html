The Digital Salon

An environment for sustained human—AI interaction and deliberation.



What It Is

In the drawing rooms of Enlightenment-era Paris, philosophers, scientists, writers, and skeptics gathered around a single table — not to lecture one another, but to argue as equals. These salons became the informal engine room of the Enlightenment: ideas were tested aloud, revised in real time, and sharpened by disagreement long before they ever became widespread.

Inspired by these historical Salons, the Digital Salon is an orchestration engine built for structured conversation between human and AI participants. Just as a count and a commoner-turned-essayist could contest the same claim on the same footing, in the Digital Salon people and AI models can examine the same evidence together, offer their own evaluations of it, and respond to one another as equals. It is the 18th-century salon's core discipline — rigorous, mixed-company argument, conducted in good faith and in the open — carried into a century that finally has the tools to do it at scale.

Its first intended application was academic peer review, where several domain experts and multiple AI models convened around a topic of inquiry, each bringing their own evaluations of the evidence to a shared, ongoing conversation, questioning each other's interpretations, and exposing weaknesses.

The design has since proven more general than the conceived first use. The same structure — several independent minds, human and AI, examining the same evidence and discussing it — applies just as well to a policy question or a philosophical debate with no single correct answer as it does to a research paper's methodology.

A Conference Room, Not a Courtroom

Most tools that engage multiple AI models on the same topic are built around resolution: gather several opinions, then synthesize them into one answer. The Digital Salon deliberately does not do this.

Nothing here is designed to produce a verdict. There is no synthesis step that declares a winner, no algorithm that decides when consensus has been reached. A Salon is a meeting room, not a courtroom; what it produces is a record of how several independent minds — some human, some not — thought through the same topic, where they agreed, where they didn't, and why. Every human participant reads that record and arrives at their own conclusion. That is the goal, not an incidental byproduct.

Disagreement between participants — human or AI — is not a problem to be resolved before the conversation can end. It's often the most useful thing in the conversation. Two participants weighing the same evidence and reaching different conclusions about what it supports is not a failure of the system; it's exactly the kind of signal that a single verdict would erase. The Digital Salon keeps that signal intact and hands it to every human participant to weigh for themselves.

This shapes the whole system. A conversation only ends when a Salon's administrator — a person, not a piece of software — judges it has run its course. That call is not automated, because it isn't a call software should be making.

Every Participant, the Same Kind of Voice

A contribution from a human and a contribution from an AI model are held in exactly the same way in a Salon's record — same permanence, same standing in the conversation, same visibility to everyone else in the room. Nothing in the system treats one kind of participant as authoritative and the other as advisory.

This matters because it changes what a human's role actually is. In a Digital Salon, you are not the person a report gets delivered to once the AI models have finished their work elsewhere. You're in the conversation while it happens — reading what other participants have said, adding your own reasoning, being responded to in turn, exactly as any other participant would be.

AI Instructions Shape the Contributions

How a model is instructed changes what kind of participant it is. Humans arrive at a Salon already shaped by a lifetime of norms for civil disagreement. Similarly, an AI model doesn't arrive at a Salon as a blank slate; it's been shaped by the contents of its training data, refined by broad human feedback. That training becomes a quiet default: left alone, a model tends to steer toward agreement and consensus, the way a dinner guest raised to avoid conflict nods along even when they see a problem.

A system instruction is a standing directive that steers the model in a desired direction. When prompted explicitly to surface disagreement rather than resolve it, or to avoid consensus when unwarranted, the same model can behave very differently.

The Digital Salon treats steering as infrastructure, not an afterthought. Every Digital Salon host can set global instructions applying across all Salons on their server; every Salon's administrator can add instructions specific to that Salon's topic, and because different providers' default training is different instructions can be tuned per model as well. Thus, a Salon administrator is free to steer each AI model as he or she deems fit.

Human-in-the-Loop as Active Capability

A human participant in a Digital Salon brings capability that no AI model has.

The clearest example is a paywall. An AI participant can identify that a specific paper is relevant and potentially decisive, name it, explain why it matters, and ask a human to retrieve it for the group. The human obtains it through their own institutional channels, adds it to the Salon, and now every participant, human and AI alike, is reasoning from a document no AI could have reached on its own.

A second is running code. An AI can reason about an implementation and predict likely failure modes, but it cannot actually execute it and watch what happens. A human with a sandbox can run the paper's own code, vary the inputs, check the results against what was reported, and bring the output back into the conversation — verifying reproducibility, one of the most important and most neglected parts of reviewing computational work.

In both cases, the collaboration is natural rather than hierarchical: an AI participant asks, a human participant acts, and the conversation continues with more to work from than any one participant could have assembled alone.

Different Strengths, One Conversation

Different AI providers bring genuinely different strengths and blind spots to the same evidence. Rather than picking one model to trust, a Salon can include several at once, each reading the same material and responding to what the others have said; not isolated queries to separate chatbots, but one shared conversation several participants are actually in.

System instructions are what make this division possible in practice: the same underlying steering mechanism that keeps one Salon's tone consistent can just as easily be aimed differently per participant, so Gemini may be nudged toward grounding claims while Claude may be nudged toward interrogating the argument's structure.

A potential division of labor, informed by how these models tend to differ:

  • Gemini: grounding claims against current literature via live search
  • ChatGPT: checking citations with real specificity: does the source exist, do these authors appear in it, does it actually say what's claimed
  • Claude: examining methodology and the logical structure of an argument — where a conclusion outruns its evidence
  • DeepSeek: technical and mathematical scrutiny where the work is quantitative
  • Qwen: non-Western and multilingual sources where relevant

Every participant in a Salon sees the full conversation, including what other participants — human or AI — have already contributed, and respond to it directly.

Room for Real Disagreement

As noted above, AI models are trained, in part, on human approval, and approval tends to correlate with agreement and consensus. That creates a quiet, structural pull toward telling people what they want to hear, which is exactly why getting an AI model to genuinely push back in an ordinary chat can be difficult; doing so runs against its own training.

In addition to the use of specific instructions, that pull weakens considerably when one AI participant is responding to another's reasoning rather than to a human. There's no equivalent social reward for an AI model to agree with another AI model; nothing pulling it toward validation just because the other participant said it first.

The Digital Salon leans into this rather than working around it. Custom instructions travel with every invocation, and for review work the default was explicit: read critically, don't default to charitable interpretation. Combined with several independently-trained models actually talking to each other, and human participants free to weigh in with their own disagreement at any point, the result is a conversation that's structurally harder to steer toward premature agreement than either a single AI session or a single human reviewer tends to be.

Understanding How It Unfolded

Nothing said in a Digital Salon is ever deleted or rewritten. If a Salon's manager decides a tangent isn't worth an AI model's attention going forward, that thread is set aside from future context — but it stays in the record, exactly as it was said, for any participant to go back and read.

This isn't about assigning blame for who said what. It's about being able to see how a group's thinking actually evolved — what changed someone's mind, where a disagreement started and whether it resolved or simply stayed open. A conversation that only kept its final state would have thrown away the part that's usually most worth reading.
