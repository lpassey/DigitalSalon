Steering AI Participants with Salon Instructions

Salon Instructions allow a Salon administrator to influence how an AI participant approaches the conversation.

AI models are not programmed in the conventional sense. They are trained on large quantities of data, including an enormous amount of human language. Through that training they learn patterns in the way people communicate: how we give instructions, explain things, make arguments, qualify statements, assign roles, express priorities, and respond to one another.

Because of this training, Large Language Models are primed to understand and interpret instructions written in ordinary human language. You tell the AI participant how you would like it to participate in the conversation. You don't write a program, specify an algorithm, or learn a special language.

This is one area where a person who is good at language may have an advantage over a programmer. Most computer programs are built by giving a computer precise instructions in a formal language. Programming rewards eliminating ambiguity: the programmer wants the computer to interpret an instruction in exactly one way, and no other.

When you attempt to steer an AI participant, natural language is the medium through which much of the model's capability was formed. That makes skills such as careful writing, close reading, understanding context, recognizing ambiguity, choosing the right emphasis, and anticipating how a statement will be understood particularly useful. These are skills that people who have spent a great deal of time reading and writing may have developed without ever thinking of them as technical skills.

Training does not end with learning language. AI providers also make deliberate choices about how their models should behave. Through additional training, system instructions, safety rules, and other forms of post-training, providers can give their models different default behaviors and personalities.

This is why the default behavior of one AI model can be quite different from another. Gemini, Mistral, and Grok, for example, may respond differently to the same conversation even when they are being asked to assess the same contribution. These differences are not necessarily the result of differences in the data used to train the models. They can reflect different choices made by their providers about how the models should behave.

It can be helpful to think of this as a kind of upbringing. A model does not arrive at the Salon with a blank slate. Before you ever give it a Salon Instruction, it has already been trained to behave in particular ways. Some models have a stronger or more distinctive behavioral orientation than others, and some models are more resistant to new instructions than others.

This is one reason Digital Salon allows, indeed encourages, Salon administrators to provide different instructions for different AI providers. You are not starting from scratch with each model. Just as with humans, you are steering a participant that already has its own learned tendencies.

None of this requires you to understand how an AI model is implemented. In fact, for the particular task of steering an AI participant, your ability to communicate clearly in ordinary language is more important than your knowledge of computers. You may be better equipped to steer an AI participant than someone whose principal expertise is software development.

If you are a Salon administrator who has never written a line of computer code, don't assume that you are at a disadvantage. You may actually be unusually well suited to this particular job.

Steering, Not Programming

An instruction can substantially influence an AI participant without guaranteeing a particular response. AI participants are no more prone to follow Salon instructions than are human participants. You are steering the model, not programming it. This means that effective steering is less about finding a magical formula for writing instructions and more about communicating clearly what you want from the model.

This distinction is important.

An AI model does not execute a Salon Instruction as a program would execute an instruction in software. The instruction becomes part of what the model is given when generating its response. The model interprets the instruction along with the conversation, its other instructions, and everything else that influences its response.

Consequently, an instruction such as:

Always challenge the conclusions of other participants.

does not mean that the model will challenge every conclusion. It may decide that a particular conclusion is well-supported and agree with it. Conversely, an instruction such as:

Do not challenge an argument unless you have a substantive objection.

may produce more selective criticism, but it cannot guarantee that the model will behave that way in every circumstance. The administrator is therefore steering, not programming, the AI participant.

Learning to steer

There is no general formula for writing Salon Instructions that will reliably produce a desired behavior. An instruction that works well with one model may work poorly with another, and an instruction that works well in one conversation may produce different results in another.

In practice, learning to steer an AI participant is largely a matter of trial and error. Write an instruction, observe the resulting contributions, and revise the instruction when the behavior is not what you intended.

This is why it can be useful to think of Salon Instructions as something closer to prompt whispering than to programming or engineering. An animal whisperer does not have a command language that determines an animal's behavior. The whisperer learns how an animal responds to different forms of guidance and uses that knowledge to influence its behavior. AI models are obviously not animals, but the analogy captures the lack of deterministic control.

Describe the behavior you want

The most useful instructions usually describe observable behavior rather than simply naming a desirable quality.

For example:

Be critical.

is vague. An administrator who wants an AI participant to identify weaknesses in an argument might instead write:

Examine claims critically. Identify unsupported assertions, missing evidence, and conclusions that do not follow from the evidence presented.

The second instruction gives the model a clearer indication of what "critical" means in this Salon. It still does not guarantee the desired behavior. Similarly:

Be independent.

could be replaced by:

Evaluate each contribution on its own merits. Do not agree with another participant merely because the participant expressed the conclusion confidently or because several participants have already agreed with it.

Again, the instruction is guidance, not a command.

How Much Instruction Is Enough?

When steering an AI participant, it is tempting to keep adding instructions until the desired behavior seems completely specified. More elaborate instructions are not necessarily more effective, however. In some cases, additional detail makes an instruction harder for the model to interpret or introduces priorities that compete with one another.

Consider the simple goal of encouraging an AI participant to evaluate other participants' conclusions independently rather than simply agreeing with them. A short instruction might be:

Do not treat agreement with another participant as evidence that the participant's conclusion is correct. Evaluate the evidence and reasoning independently.

This is concise and gives the model a clear behavioral objective. It may be all that is needed.

A more ambitious instruction could make the desired behavior more explicit:

Evaluate each participant's conclusion on its own merits. Do not accept a conclusion merely because another participant has expressed it confidently or because several participants agree. Examine the evidence and reasoning supporting the conclusion, and identify any important assumptions or unsupported inferences. If the conclusion is well supported, say so rather than inventing an objection.

This gives the model several related behaviors to consider. It may produce more consistent results, particularly when the administrator has observed a specific problem that the shorter instruction did not address.

It is also possible to go too far:

Evaluate every conclusion independently and never accept another participant's conclusion without first identifying all assumptions, examining every premise, considering alternative explanations, looking for possible counterexamples, distinguishing factual claims from inferences, assessing the quality and independence of the evidence, determining whether other participants have relied upon the same sources or assumptions, and explicitly reporting every substantive reason for agreement or disagreement. You must remain skeptical even when the evidence appears conclusive, and you should challenge any conclusion that could possibly be questioned.

This instruction sounds thorough, but thoroughness is not necessarily a virtue in a Salon Instruction. It contains many separate requirements, some of which may compete with one another. "Remain skeptical even when the evidence appears conclusive," for example, can encourage the model to manufacture objections when the evidence really does support a conclusion. The instruction may also cause the model to spend much of its response explaining its evaluation process rather than contributing to the conversation.

The lesson is not that short instructions are always better. It is that an instruction should be no more elaborate than necessary to produce the behavior you actually want.

If an instruction is not producing the desired behavior, adding more words is only one possible response. A better approach may be to identify the specific behavior that is missing and describe that behavior more precisely. The administrator can then observe the result and revise the instruction again.

In this sense, effective steering is empirical. There is no reliable formula that tells an administrator how much instruction a particular model needs. The model's behavior provides the evidence, and the instruction can be adjusted accordingly.

Give a participant a particular role

A Salon may benefit from giving different AI participants different responsibilities. For example, one participant might be asked to concentrate on evidence:

Examine factual claims carefully. Distinguish what is established by the available evidence from what is inferred from that evidence. Identify important claims that require additional evidence.

Another might concentrate on reasoning:

Examine the logical structure of the arguments presented by participants. Identify assumptions, unsupported inferences, and conclusions that do not follow from the premises.

Another might deliberately look for alternatives:

When a participant presents a proposed explanation, consider whether there are other plausible explanations that could account for the same evidence.

These instructions do not prevent the participants from discussing other matters. They give each participant a particular emphasis.

Do not confuse skepticism with disagreement

An administrator attempting to reduce AI agreeableness might initially write:

Always disagree with other participants.

That may produce disagreement, but it is probably not the behavior actually wanted. A model following such guidance could manufacture objections simply because disagreement was requested. A more useful instruction might be:

Do not assume that another participant's conclusion is correct. Look for substantive reasons to question it. If the conclusion is well-supported, acknowledge that rather than inventing an objection.

The distinction illustrates an important aspect of steering: instructions can have unintended consequences. Making an instruction stronger does not necessarily make the resulting behavior better.

Instructions can establish priorities

Sometimes the desired behavior involves choosing between competing objectives. For example:

Accuracy is more important than completeness. When important information is uncertain or unavailable, identify the uncertainty rather than filling the gap with speculation.

Or:

Preserve genuine disagreements. Do not attempt to create consensus merely because several participants have expressed similar views.

Such instructions tell the model what the administrator considers more important when the desired behaviors compete.

Different Kinds of Conversation

Salon Instructions can be used to steer an AI participant toward the kind of conversation the Salon is intended to have. The same general instructions can therefore produce quite different behavior when a Salon has a different purpose.

A brainstorming Salon, for example, can instruct participants to favor generating and extending ideas over evaluating them:

Prioritize generating and extending ideas over evaluating them; a promising idea's flaws can be examined later, a discarded idea's potential often cannot be recovered.

A clinical discussion can instead tell participants exactly what kind of analysis is wanted:

Reason about the case as presented: differential diagnosis, what evidence supports or weakens each possibility, what additional information would discriminate between them.

A philosophy Salon can establish a different conversational objective altogether:

The goal is not resolution. Do not manufacture a synthesis where none is warranted, and do not soften a genuine disagreement into a false middle ground.

Notice that none of these instructions tells the AI participant what conclusion to reach. They tell it what kind of contribution is appropriate to the conversation.

This is one of the most useful things Salon Instructions can do: establish the character and priorities of a Salon without attempting to prescribe everything an AI participant should say.

Different models may respond differently

Digital Salon permits provider-specific Salon Instructions because the same instruction need not produce the same behavior from every AI model.

An instruction that produces useful criticism from one model may produce excessive criticism from another. A model may respond particularly well to short, direct instructions, while another may respond better to a more detailed description of the desired behavior.

This is another reason that steering is empirical. There is no substitute for observing what a particular model actually does.

There are limits to steering

Salon Instructions cannot be assumed to override other instructions or restrictions governing an AI participant. Nor can they guarantee a particular answer, personality, reasoning process, or level of cooperation. An administrator should therefore be cautious about writing instructions containing words such as "always," "never," or "must." Such words can be appropriate when describing a genuine priority, but they do not turn an instruction into an enforceable rule.

For example:

Never speculate.

may sound precise, but it does not necessarily tell the model what to do when the available evidence is incomplete. A more useful instruction might be:

When evidence is incomplete, distinguish clearly between what is established and what is speculative. Do not present a speculation as an established fact.

The latter describes the desired behavior rather than merely demanding an outcome.

The practical objective

The purpose of Salon Instructions is not to make an AI participant predictable. It is to give the participant a useful orientation within the conversation.

A good instruction may encourage a model to examine evidence more carefully, challenge weak reasoning, preserve disagreement, or concentrate on a particular aspect of a problem. The model may nevertheless behave differently from what the administrator expected. That is not necessarily a failure of the Salon Instructions. It is an inherent property of steering a generative AI model.

The administrator's task is to formulate useful guidance, observe its effects, and learn from the results.
